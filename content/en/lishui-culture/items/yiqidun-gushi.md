## Name and category

According to the public notice of January 2024, 17 projects were recommended for the fourth batch of district-level intangible cultural heritage items in Lishui District, of which 14 were new inscriptions and three extended projects. The Tale of Yiqidun is one of the new inscriptions and belongs to the folk-literature category; it is the only folk-literature item among the 17 projects, and its recommended guardian is the Hefeng Town Cultural and Sports Centre.

## Place of transmission

The Yiqidun that appears in the item title is in Zhongyang Village, Hefeng Town. The heritage status and level of the mound itself are recorded separately in this library's history section, under the Yiqidun entry (the municipal list and the district's third-batch list differ as to its date and level). This entry does not repeat that record, and it does not infer the level of the tale from the mound's protected status.

## The story, as a folk legend

According to a 2014 China News Service report carried from the Jinling Evening News, local tradition in Lishui holds that the Yiqidun mound is the joint tomb of Zuo Botao and Yang Jiao'ai: the two were men of Yan who set out south when King of Chu called for talents; reaching Lishui, they were overcome by cold and hunger, and Zuo Botao, to save his friend, left him the provisions and shut himself in a hollow tree to die. Yang Jiao'ai later became a grandee of Chu and dreamed that Zuo Botao was tormented by evil spirits in the nether world, whereupon he took his own life to go and help him. The report says the idiom "to accompany a gentleman at the cost of one's life" originates here.

The above is a folk legend. This entry keeps its legendary character, sets it apart from historical statement, and does not write it as fact.

## Where legend and history part

The same report also carries the specialists' view: there are several "joint tombs of Zuo Botao and Yang Jiao'ai" in China, and the Lishui mound is very likely a mound tomb in origin, the local people having attached the moving tale to it and so produced a relic. On this view, the claim that Yiqidun is the joint tomb of the two is an attachment, and the mound at the level of documented history still rests on its archaeology and on the mound itself. This entry sets the legend and the specialists' view side by side and does not choose between them.

## Points in doubt

First, only the outline of the story as retold in a media report is available; the teller, the range of transmission and the occasions of telling are not recorded and remain to be added.

Second, whether Yiqidun is the joint tomb of the two is doubted by the specialists cited in the report; this entry sets out both accounts and does not raise confidence on that account.

Third, the date and heritage level of the Yiqidun mound are recorded under the Yiqidun entry of this library's history section; this entry does not repeat them.

Fourth, the reporting-area column for this row in the public notice reads "Qinhuai District", which does not agree with the place of transmission in Hefeng Town and is inconsistent with the other rows; the wording is reproduced as it stands, not silently altered, and the discrepancy remains to be verified.

Fifth, this library searched the full texts of the five Lishui County Gazetteers it holds and found no passage on Yiqidun, Zuo Botao or Yang Jiao'ai; the legend has no gazetteer basis, so no starting date is given.

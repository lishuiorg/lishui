Qi Tai was a native of Lishui. The *Ming Shi*, juan 141 (Biographies 29), carries his life; the Kangxi *Lishui County Gazetteer*, juan 6, has a longer life of its own in its category of loyal martyrs, and the Guangxu gazetteer, juan 8, records his shrine. The two lives disagree on dates. This entry quotes each source as it stands and sets the disagreements side by side, adding nothing neither carries.

## Life

The *Ming Shi* gives his original name and his examinations:

> 齐泰，溧水人。初名德。洪武十七年，举应天乡试第一。明年成进士。历礼、兵二部主事。雷震谨身殿，太祖祷郊庙，择历官九年无过者陪祀，德与焉，赐名泰。二十八年，以兵部郎中擢左侍郎。

The Kangxi gazetteer's life in juan 6 gives his names and examinations differently:

> 齐泰，原名德，字尚礼，别号南塘。洪武二十年举应天乡试第一，明年举进士，授礼部主事，寻改兵部。会雷震谨身殿，上祷郊庙，以泰官九载无过，得陪祀，赐名泰。三十年升本部左侍郎，明年进尚书。

The two differ in three ways, and this entry records both. First, the *Ming Shi* says only "originally named De", while the gazetteer adds a courtesy name Shangli and a style name Nantang; everything this library knows of his courtesy and style names comes from the gazetteer alone. Second, the *Ming Shi* places his first-place result in the Yingtian provincial examination in 1384 and the *jinshi* degree in 1385, while the gazetteer places the examination in 1387. Third, the *Ming Shi* places his promotion to the vice-ministry in 1395, from a secretaryship in the Ministry of War, while the gazetteer places it in 1397 and gives no office he was promoted from. This entry does not decide which dating is right.

The gazetteer's life also records his receipt of the deathbed charge: the Jianwen emperor asked him the names of the frontier commanders, he recited them all without omission, was asked for the records, drew a booklet from his sleeve and presented it, greatly pleasing the emperor, and "in that year received the deathbed charge". The *Ming Shi* does not record this; it is entered here on the authority of the gazetteer.

On the Jianwen emperor's accession the heir apparent, later the Chengzu emperor, had long valued him, and ordered him together with Huang Zicheng to participate in state affairs; he was subsequently advanced to Minister of War. As the Yan forces pressed on the capital the Jianwen emperor recalled him, but he had not arrived when the capital fell.

> 迨燕兵日逼，复召泰还。未至，京师已不守，泰走外郡谋兴复。时购泰急。泰墨白马走，行稍远，汗出墨脱。或曰：「此齐尚书马也。」遂被执赴京，同子澄、方孝孺不屈死。

He fled to the outer commanderies to plot a restoration. The hunt for him was urgent. He rode a white horse blackened with ink; when he had gone some way the sweat ran and the ink came off, and someone said "That is Minister Qi's horse." He was seized and taken to the capital, and died unsubmitting together with Huang Zicheng and Fang Xiaoru. His cousins such as Jingzong were executed with him; his uncles Shi Yong, Yang Yan and others were sentenced to exile. His son, six years old, was spared death and given over to be enslaved.

The Kangxi gazetteer, in its annals for 1425, gives only the background:

> 仁宗洪熙元年乙巳，赦溧水前兵部尚书齐泰宗党，给还故业。齐泰死建文君难，详《传》。

The "former" before "Minister of War" shows that the office recorded is the one he held under the Jianwen emperor. The gazetteer says the details of his death are "in the biography", but that volume contains no biographies, so the details must come from the *Ming Shi* — which is where the two sources complement each other.

## Three measures after his death

The first came in 1425, when his kin were pardoned and the family property restored. The *Ming Shi* records only that his son was "pardoned and sent home in the Renzong reign". The two sources cover the same period but differ in scope: the gazetteer speaks of the kin and the former property, the history only of the son. This entry sets them side by side without choosing.

The second came in 1525, when the magistrate Wang Congshan asked that Qi Tai be sacrificed to in Lishui, the shrine being named Biaozhong. The Guangxu gazetteer adds the site and later history:

> 表忠祠在縣北門外望京街，祀明兵部尚書齊泰。嘉靖四年，詔復爵，贈謚節愍，命立祠，春秋致祭。知縣王從善建，扁其堂曰勁草，詔予蔭襲奉祀生員一人。二十年，知縣謝廷茝重修祠宇，置義田十畝。

The shrine stood outside the north gate of the county on Wangjing Street, and its hall was named Jincao ("hard grass"). Wang Congshan built it; in 1541 the magistrate Xie Tingchai rebuilt it and endowed ten mu of charitable land. The Guangxu gazetteer places the "restoration of rank and posthumous name Jieming" in 1525, while the Kangxi gazetteer speaks only of the magistrate's request; the common account places the posthumous name in the Southern Ming Hongguang period. The two datings are set side by side.

The third came in 1621, when the court pardoned the households of Qi Tai's kin exiled with him and returned them to the Lishui registers. The number differs within the same book: the Kangxi gazetteer's annals for 1621 say thirty-eight households; its life of Qi Tai in juan 6 says "in 1621 the magistrate Zhang Ximing, supported by Xu Bida, prefect of Yingtian, and the censor Tian, obtained leave to exempt the forty-five households of Yang Baoyuan, Luo Yingpeng and others, who returned to their registers and their property"; and its biography of Xu Bida says "the county men Yang Baoyuan, Luo Yingpeng and others were implicated as kin of Qi Tai and enrolled as forty-five garrison households". Thirty-eight and forty-five are both recorded here and neither is chosen. Between the pardon of 1425 and the restoration of 1621 lie 196 years; Qi Tai's standing in Lishui was established through these three measures in sequence.

## A failed petition and the record of his descendants

The gazetteer's life records a fourth, unsuccessful measure: in 1584 the censor Tu Shufan invoked an edict of favour to ask for the pardon of Qi Tai's exiled kin, and "there was no reply". The annals do not record this; it is the only failed petition between the shrine of 1525 and the exemption of 1621 known to this library.

The same passage records that in the Jiajing era the magistrates Wang Congshan and Xie Ting successively arranged sacrificial rites for him, and that they asked that his fifth-generation descendant Guangyu be enrolled as a student to keep the shrine, adding that "Guangyu was the descendant of the child of six". That last phrase links him to the six-year-old son the *Ming Shi* says was spared. Qi Tai's great-grandfather Yingfu, grandfather Ze and father Zhongrong were "given ranks corresponding to Qi Tai's rank". His cult in Lishui and the schooling and property of his descendants were arranged together. The name of the magistrate appears as Xie Ting in the Kangxi gazetteer's officials' biographies and as Xie Tingchai in the Guangxu gazetteer's cults section; both are recorded.

The same passage adds "ten li east of the county there is Shangshu Pu, which was Qi Tai's house". This is the only gazetteer statement known to this library placing Qi Tai's house at a specific point in Lishui. Whether "Shangshu Pu" survives as a place name and where it lay are not recorded in this library's sources, and no location is given here.

## Relation to Lishui

His native place is given in the *Ming Shi* as Lishui. The 1425 pardon names him "Qi Tai of Lishui, formerly Minister of War", linking him directly to his home county and to the property there. From the 1525 shrine onward the county sacrificed to him as a loyal minister. He enters this library as a Lishui native.

## Points of doubt

First, his birth and death years are unknown. The *Ming Shi* gives the examinations of 1384 and 1385, the promotion of 1395, and his death during the Jingnan Campaign, but no year of death; this entry does not infer one. His courtesy name Shangli and style name Nantang appear only in the Kangxi gazetteer; the *Ming Shi* gives only "originally named De", so the two accounts carry unequal information about his names.

Second, the dates of his examinations and promotion differ between the two accounts and cannot be resolved within this entry. The entry therefore never states a single year for them, only "the *Ming Shi* says X, the gazetteer says Y".

Third, the gazetteer's annals refer the circumstances of his death to a biography not present in the volume consulted, so this entry takes the ink-blackened horse and the arrest from the *Ming Shi*. The gazetteer's own life in juan 6 tells a different ending: Qi Tai fled towards Guangde, met the compiler Wang Shuying, the two took leave of each other, Wang Shuying killed himself, and Qi Tai raised troops in other commanderies before being seized and brought before the Chengzu emperor, dying unsubmitting. The *Ming Shi* records flight to the outer commanderies and recognition by the ink. The two accounts differ in route and detail and are set side by side, not merged into one narrative.

Fourth, the *Ming Shi* says the naming followed the choice of faultless nine-year officials for attendance at the suburban rites, but it does not state the causal link between the attendance and the grant of a name.

Fifth, the scope of the 1425 pardon and the manner of the property restoration are not consistent between the two sources.

Sixth, the year of the posthumous name Jieming differs: the Guangxu gazetteer places it in 1525, the common account in the Southern Ming Hongguang period, while the Kangxi gazetteer's life states the posthumous name and the shrine without giving a year. Three accounts of unequal precision are set side by side.

Seventh, on the households pardoned in 1621 the same gazetteer gives thirty-eight in its annals and forty-five in two passages of juan 6; both are recorded. It does not say whether they were those missed in 1425, nor where they were sent or for how long.

Eighth, across the seven layers of sources searched: the official history has a life; the Kangxi gazetteer has a life in its loyal-martyrs category, two entries in its annals and one related entry in its officials' biographies; the Guangxu gazetteer has one entry in its cults section. That is enough to establish the framework of his life and the three measures in Lishui. But his house, lineage and landed property in Lishui are recorded in none of them beyond the single sentence about Shangshu Pu, and this entry makes no inference. Juan 6 of the Kangxi gazetteer was read from an online transcription and has not been checked against a printed edition.

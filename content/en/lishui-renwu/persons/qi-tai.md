Qi Tai was a native of Lishui. The *Ming Shi*, juan 141 (Biographies 29), carries his life, which is the most complete account of him the library knows; the Kangxi and Guangxu *Lishui County Gazetteers* record what happened to him in the county after his death. This entry is built from those two kinds of source and adds no detail the gazetteers do not carry.

## Life

The *Ming Shi* gives his original name and his examinations:

> 齐泰，溧水人。初名德。洪武十七年，举应天乡试第一。明年成进士。历礼、兵二部主事。雷震谨身殿，太祖祷郊庙，择历官九年无过者陪祀，德与焉，赐名泰。二十八年，以兵部郎中擢左侍郎。

He was originally named De. He took first place in the Yingtian provincial examination in 1384 and passed the *jinshi* examination in 1385. He served as a secretary in the Ministry of Rites and then the Ministry of War. When lightning struck the Jinshen Hall, the founding emperor prayed at the suburban altars and the ancestral temple and chose for attendance those officials who had served nine years without fault; De was among them, and was granted the name Tai. In 1395 he was promoted from a secretaryship in the Ministry of War to the vice-ministry.

On the Jianwen emperor's accession the heir apparent, later the Chengzu emperor, had long valued him, and ordered him together with Huang Zicheng to participate in state affairs; he was subsequently advanced to Minister of War. As the Yan forces pressed on the capital the Jianwen emperor recalled him, but he had not arrived when the capital fell.

> 迨燕兵日逼，复召泰还。未至，京师已不守，泰走外郡谋兴复。时购泰急。泰墨白马走，行稍远，汗出墨脱。或曰：「此齐尚书马也。」遂被执赴京，同子澄、方孝孺不屈死。

He fled to the outer commanderies to plot a restoration. The hunt for him was urgent. He rode a white horse blackened with ink; when he had gone some way the sweat ran and the ink came off, and someone said "That is Minister Qi's horse." He was seized and taken to the capital, and died unsubmitting together with Huang Zicheng and Fang Xiaoru. His cousins such as Jingzong were executed with him; his uncles Shi Yong, Yang Yan and others were sentenced to exile. His son, six years old, was spared death and given over to be enslaved.

The Kangxi gazetteer, in its annals for 1425, gives only the background:

> 仁宗洪熙元年乙巳，赦溧水前兵部尚书齐泰宗党，给还故业。齐泰死建文君难，详《传》。

The "former" before "Minister of War" shows that the office recorded is the one he held under the Jianwen emperor. The gazetteer says the details of his death are "in the biography", but that volume contains no biographies, so the details must come from the *Ming Shi* — which is where the two sources complement each other.

## Three measures after his death

The first came in 1425, when his kin were pardoned and the family property restored. The *Ming Shi* records only that his son was "pardoned and sent home in the Renzong reign". The two sources cover the same period but differ in scope: the gazetteer speaks of the kin and the former property, the history only of the son. This entry sets them side by side without choosing.

The second came in 1525, when the magistrate Wang Congshan asked that Qi Tai be sacrificed to in Lishui, the shrine being named Biaozhong. The Guangxu gazetteer adds the site and later history:

> 表忠祠在縣北門外望京街，祀明兵部尚書齊泰。嘉靖四年，詔復爵，贈謚節愍，命立祠，春秋致祭。知縣王從善建，扁其堂曰勁草，詔予蔭襲奉祀生員一人。二十年，知縣謝廷茝重修祠宇，置義田十畝。

The shrine stood outside the north gate of the county on Wangjing Street, and its hall was named Jincao ("hard grass"). Wang Congshan built it; in 1541 the magistrate Xie Tingchai rebuilt it and endowed ten mu of charitable land. The Guangxu gazetteer places the "restoration of rank and posthumous name Jieming" in 1525, while the Kangxi gazetteer speaks only of the magistrate's request; the common account places the posthumous name in the Southern Ming Hongguang period. The two datings are set side by side.

The third came in 1621, when the court pardoned the thirty-eight households of Qi Tai's kin that had been exiled with him and returned them to the Lishui registers. Between the pardon of 1425 and the restoration of 1621 lie 196 years; Qi Tai's standing in Lishui was established through these three measures in sequence.

## Relation to Lishui

His native place is given in the *Ming Shi* as Lishui. The 1425 pardon names him "Qi Tai of Lishui, formerly Minister of War", linking him directly to his home county and to the property there. From the 1525 shrine onward the county sacrificed to him as a loyal minister. He enters this library as a Lishui native.

## Points of doubt

First, his birth and death years are unknown. The *Ming Shi* gives the examinations of 1384 and 1385, the promotion of 1395, and his death during the Jingnan Campaign, but no year of death; this entry does not infer one.

Second, the gazetteer refers the circumstances of his death to a biography not present in the volume consulted; this entry supplies the ink-blackened horse and the unsubmitting death from the *Ming Shi* and adds nothing beyond it.

Third, the *Ming Shi* says the naming followed the choice of faultless nine-year officials for attendance at the suburban rites, but it does not state the causal link between the attendance and the grant of a name.

Fourth, the scope of the 1425 pardon and the manner of the property restoration are not consistent between the two sources.

Fifth, the year of the posthumous name Jieming differs: the Guangxu gazetteer places it in 1525, the common account in the Southern Ming Hongguang period. No independent Ming-period account of the Lishui Biaozhong shrine was found.

Sixth, the gazetteer does not say whether the thirty-eight households pardoned in 1621 were those missed in 1425, nor where they were sent or for how long.

Seventh, across the seven layers of sources searched: the official history has a life, and the two county gazetteers record the 1425 pardon and the history of the shrine. That is enough to establish the framework of his life. But his house, lineage and landed property in Lishui are recorded in none of the three, and this entry makes no inference about them.

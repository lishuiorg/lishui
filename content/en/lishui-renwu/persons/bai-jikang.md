Bai Jikang is an entry in the Tang part of the notable-officials biography in juan 6 of the Kangxi *Lishui County Gazetteer*, the longest of the Tang entries in this volume. It is also, in this library's knowledge, the earliest gazetteer text that identifies the city god of Lishui with a named Tang official of the county.

## Origin and term

> 白季康，太原人。父麟，为扬州录事。母梦感白蛇而孕生。其先，秦武安君起，数传至公。
> —— 康熙《溧水县志》卷之六·名宦传

He was a native of Taiyuan; his father Lin served as a registrar of Yangzhou. "His mother conceived him after a dream in which she was affected by a white snake" is a birth legend recorded by the gazetteer, and "his ancestors go back to Qi, the Lord of Wuan of Qin, and descended several generations to him" is a lineage claim recorded by the gazetteer. Both are quoted here with their character marked, and neither is stated as fact. No year of appointment is recorded.

## What the gazetteer records

> 生而温恭信厚，为官贞白严重。始任华州下邳尉，及历宦溧水，已四为令矣，而竟卒于官。
> —— 康熙《溧水县志》卷之六·名宦传

He began as constable of Xiapi in Huazhou, and the gazetteer says that when he came to Lishui he had already held the office of "ling" four times. From this we know he had served as a constable before Lishui and had been a magistrate four times over; but the gazetteer records neither where the other three posts were nor which of the four Lishui was. It does not itself give the formal title of his Lishui post, but the epitaph carried in juan 8 of the same gazetteer names it as magistrate of Lishui in Xuanzhou (see below). It records that he "in the end died in post". "Gentle, respectful, trustworthy and generous from birth; upright, pure and grave as an official" is the gazetteer's assessment.

> 邑民思而俎豆之不衰，遂为神，即本邑城隍是也。迄今禳祷辄应，邑民尊崇之，视他邑特异焉。
> —— 康熙《溧水县志》卷之六·名宦传

"He became a god, namely this county's city god" is the earliest gazetteer text known to this library identifying the city god of Lishui with Bai Jikang. It is a religious statement made by the gazetteer; it is quoted here with that character marked, and not stated as history. "To this day prayers and averting rites are answered" and "the people of the county honour him in a way unlike other counties" belong to the same order of statement, and the "today" in them means the time of the Kangxi compilation. This entry draws no conclusion from them about the site, foundation or history of the city-god institution in Lishui.

> 公之生平详于从子居易墓志，有云『公才不偶时，道屈于位，命矣。』曷知其（快快）［怏怏］未试之志，犹炳烺千禩而不灭哉？
> —— 康熙《溧水县志》卷之六·名宦传

The gazetteer says the details of his life are set out in the funerary inscription written by "his nephew Juyi", and quotes that inscription: "his talent did not meet the times, his way was bent by his position — such was his fate." "Nephew" (a cousin's son) is the kinship term the gazetteer itself uses. This is the only clue known to this library connecting Bai Juyi with Bai Jikang, and it is of the order "an inscription written for a kinsman": it does not show that Bai Juyi ever came to Lishui. Here the biography quotes only this one sentence; the full text is carried in juan 8 ("Literature") of the same gazetteer, and a later section of this entry draws on it. In the transcription the passage reads "（快快）［怏怏］"; the characters inside the square brackets, "怏怏", are the transcription's own correction, and this entry uses them.

## The epitaph recorded in juan 8 of the Kangxi gazetteer

juan 8 of the Kangxi *Lishui County Gazetteer* ("Literature") carries Bai Juyi's "Epitaph for Lord Bai" (headed "Grand Tutor"), one of two redactions of the same epitaph also found in juan 70 of the *Bai shi changqing ji*. The notable-officials biography says his life is "set out in detail in the inscription by his nephew Juyi", meaning this text; the points below are added from it.

Post: the epitaph names his last office as magistrate of Lishui in Xuanzhou, the formal title of his Lishui post, which the biography's "served in office at Lishui" leaves unstated. Lineage: it names his great-grandfather Shitong, governor of Lizhou; his grandfather Zhishan, imperial physician; and his father Lin, registrar of Yangzhou, the last agreeing with the biography. Career: it lists posts at Xiaqui in Huazhou, Henei in Huaizhou, Pengcheng in Xuzhou, Xunyang in Jiangzhou, Hongxian in Suzhou and Lishui in Xuanzhou; the last four are all "ling" (magistracies) with Lishui last, and these are the four the biography means by "already a magistrate four times over". Household: his first wife, née Xue, bore two sons, constables of Yuqian in Hangzhou and Suian in Muzhou, and a daughter, Jianxu, who took vows before marriage; his second wife, née Jing of Gaoyang, bore his son Minzhong, a presented scholar. Burial: it says he "died in the official residence" and was buried back at Xiaqui; his second wife died in the seventh year of Taihe (833) and was interred with him the next year.

One orthographic difference: the place of burial is written 下邽 in the epitaph but 下邳 in the biography; the two are set side by side, and this entry does not decide between them.

One difference of address: the biography calls Juyi Jikang's "congzi" (nephew), while the epitaph has Jikang's son Minzhong "entrust the stone to his cousin Juyi"; the two terms differ and are set side by side without adjudication.

## Relation to Lishui

The gazetteer records that he "served in office at Lishui" and "died in post", and this entry records him through official service. The epitaph carried in juan 8 of the same gazetteer names his last post as magistrate of Lishui in Xuanzhou, the formal title of his Lishui office. His most important connection with Lishui is posthumous: the gazetteer makes him this county's city god, with the people sacrificing to him without ceasing. That passage is, in this library's knowledge, the earliest text linking the city god of Lishui to a named Tang official, and its character is marked in the section above.

## Points of doubt

First, his birth and death years are not recorded.

Second, no year of appointment is recorded; the biography never states the formal title of his Lishui post, but the epitaph carried in juan 8 of the same gazetteer names it as magistrate of Lishui in Xuanzhou, and this entry therefore uses "ling" (magistrate), not the later term "zhixian".

Third, the white-snake conception is a legend recorded by the gazetteer and the descent from the Lord of Wuan of Qin is a lineage claim recorded by it; neither is treated as fact.

Fourth, "he became a god, namely this county's city god" and "prayers are answered to this day" are religious statements of the gazetteer, quoted with that character marked; the site, founding date and history of the city god of Lishui are not settled from them.

Fifth, the biography does not record the order of the four magisterial posts or the locations of the other three; the epitaph carried in juan 8 lists them as Pengcheng in Xuzhou, Xunyang in Jiangzhou, Hongxian in Suzhou and Lishui in Xuanzhou, with Lishui last, though the years of each remain unknown.

Sixth, the biography only quotes one sentence of the inscription by "his nephew Juyi"; its full text is carried in juan 8 ("Literature") of the same gazetteer, and this entry uses it, though nothing about any direct contact between Bai Juyi and Lishui is inferred from it.

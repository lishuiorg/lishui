Bai Jikang is an entry in the Tang part of the notable-officials biography in juan 6 of the Kangxi *Lishui County Gazetteer*, the longest of the Tang entries in this volume. It is also, in this library's knowledge, the earliest gazetteer text that identifies the city god of Lishui with a named Tang official of the county.

## Origin and term

> 白季康，太原人。父麟，为扬州录事。母梦感白蛇而孕生。其先，秦武安君起，数传至公。
> —— 康熙《溧水县志》卷之六·名宦传

He was a native of Taiyuan; his father Lin served as a registrar of Yangzhou. "His mother conceived him after a dream in which she was affected by a white snake" is a birth legend recorded by the gazetteer, and "his ancestors go back to Qi, the Lord of Wuan of Qin, and descended several generations to him" is a lineage claim recorded by the gazetteer. Both are quoted here with their character marked, and neither is stated as fact. No year of appointment is recorded.

## What the gazetteer records

> 生而温恭信厚，为官贞白严重。始任华州下邳尉，及历宦溧水，已四为令矣，而竟卒于官。
> —— 康熙《溧水县志》卷之六·名宦传

He began as constable of Xiapi in Huazhou, and the gazetteer says that when he came to Lishui he had already held the office of "ling" four times. From this we know he had served as a constable before Lishui and had been a magistrate four times over; but the gazetteer records neither where the other three posts were nor which of the four Lishui was, and it never states the formal title of his Lishui post. It records that he "in the end died in post". "Gentle, respectful, trustworthy and generous from birth; upright, pure and grave as an official" is the gazetteer's assessment.

> 邑民思而俎豆之不衰，遂为神，即本邑城隍是也。迄今禳祷辄应，邑民尊崇之，视他邑特异焉。
> —— 康熙《溧水县志》卷之六·名宦传

"He became a god, namely this county's city god" is the earliest gazetteer text known to this library identifying the city god of Lishui with Bai Jikang. It is a religious statement made by the gazetteer; it is quoted here with that character marked, and not stated as history. "To this day prayers and averting rites are answered" and "the people of the county honour him in a way unlike other counties" belong to the same order of statement, and the "today" in them means the time of the Kangxi compilation. This entry draws no conclusion from them about the site, foundation or history of the city-god institution in Lishui.

> 公之生平详于从子居易墓志，有云『公才不偶时，道屈于位，命矣。』曷知其（快快）［怏怏］未试之志，犹炳烺千禩而不灭哉？
> —— 康熙《溧水县志》卷之六·名宦传

The gazetteer says the details of his life are set out in the funerary inscription written by "his nephew Juyi", and quotes that inscription: "his talent did not meet the times, his way was bent by his position — such was his fate." "Nephew" (a cousin's son) is the kinship term the gazetteer itself uses. This is the only clue known to this library connecting Bai Juyi with Bai Jikang, and it is of the order "an inscription written for a kinsman": it does not show that Bai Juyi ever came to Lishui. Whether that inscription survives, and what its full text is, is not recorded — the gazetteer quotes only this one sentence, and this entry goes no further. In the transcription the passage reads "（快快）［怏怏］"; the characters inside the square brackets, "怏怏", are the transcription's own correction, and this entry uses them.

## Relation to Lishui

The gazetteer records that he "served in office at Lishui" and "died in post", and this entry records him through official service. His most important connection with Lishui is posthumous: the gazetteer makes him this county's city god, with the people sacrificing to him without ceasing. That passage is, in this library's knowledge, the earliest text linking the city god of Lishui to a named Tang official, and its character is marked in the section above.

## Points of doubt

First, his birth and death years are not recorded.

Second, no year of appointment is recorded, and the gazetteer never states the formal title of his Lishui post; this entry does not render it as "magistrate of the county".

Third, the white-snake conception is a legend recorded by the gazetteer and the descent from the Lord of Wuan of Qin is a lineage claim recorded by it; neither is treated as fact.

Fourth, "he became a god, namely this county's city god" and "prayers are answered to this day" are religious statements of the gazetteer, quoted with that character marked; the site, founding date and history of the city god of Lishui are not settled from them.

Fifth, the order of the four magisterial posts and the locations of the other three are not recorded.

Sixth, the survival and full text of the inscription by "his nephew Juyi" are not recorded, and nothing about any direct contact between Bai Juyi and Lishui is inferred from it.

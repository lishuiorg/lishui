Wang Congshan was a Ming magistrate who served in Lishui. The backbone of his life comes from a single entry in his official biography in juan 6 of the Kangxi *Lishui County Gazetteer*; his acts in Lishui appear scattered across three other parts of the same book, with the Guangxu gazetteer and the *Jiangnan Tongzhi* adding more. This entry keeps the biography as its frame and reports the rest source by source rather than merging them into one account.

## Name and origin

His biography opens with his courtesy name, native place and route into office:

> 王從善，字承吉，襄陽人。由進士，嘉靖三年任。藹然愛民而老於吏治。夙富文詞，政舉其要。
> — Kangxi *Lishui County Gazetteer*, juan 6

He was courtesy-named Chengji, a native of Xiangyang, entered office through the *jinshi* degree, and took up the magistracy of Lishui in 1524. The biography's remarks that he was "gentle and loving toward the people, seasoned in administration" and "rich in literary gifts, and grasped what was essential in governance" are the gazetteer's judgement of his conduct, not a record of fact; they are quoted as such.

The Nanjing Wuxiang Mountain scenic area's own website gives him the additional style name Fenglin, and the cliff section is therefore called "Fenglin cliff inscriptions". His biography in the gazetteer records only his courtesy name, not a style name; the style name is known to this library only from the scenic area and the media, and the two are set side by side.

## Acts in Lishui

**Securing sacrifices for Qi Tai.** The annals for 1525 in juan 1 record:

> 四年乙酉，知县王从善请祀齐泰于溧水，祠曰「表忠」。申免抛荒粮米。

His biography says he "first requested a shrine of loyal integrity for Qi, posthumously named Jieming". The two passages in the same book describe one act, but the name of the shrine differs — Biaozhong ("expressing loyalty") in the annals, Zhongjie ("loyal integrity") in the biography. This entry keeps both without deciding. The Guangxu gazetteer adds the site and the hall name, and attributes the building to Wang Congshan:

> 表忠祠在縣北門外望京街，祀明兵部尚書齊泰。嘉靖四年，詔復爵，贈謚節愍，命立祠，春秋致祭。知縣王從善建，扁其堂曰勁草，詔予蔭襲奉祀生員一人。
> — Guangxu *Lishui County Gazetteer*, juan 8

The shrine stood outside the north gate on Wangjing Street, and its hall was named Jincao. The Guangxu gazetteer places an imperial order restoring his rank and conferring the posthumous name Jieming in 1525, while the Kangxi gazetteer records only a magistrate's request. The two differ on who took the initiative — an imperial order or a local petition — and this difference is recorded here and in the entry on Qi Tai without choosing.

**Building a granary.** His biography records that he "built the Xiansi granary, for use in relief". The name is given; the site, capacity and actual operation are not.

**Reviving the community oath.** Juan 3 of the gazetteer has a separate section on the community oath (*xiangyue*) recording its rise and fall in Lishui, with Wang Congshan as the man who revived it in the Jiajing era:

> 岁久弛废，虚名徒存，不知敦俗化民此其首务。嘉靖间知县王从善行之，后无继者。
> — Kangxi *Lishui County Gazetteer*, juan 3

By then the oath had long lapsed into "an empty name". He put it back into practice, but no one continued it. The same section records its revival under the magistrate Fu Yingzhen in the Wanli era and again under Chen Ruyi in 1638, so what he revived did not become a standing institution in Lishui.

**Revising the county gazetteer.** His biography says he "compiled and revised the county gazetteer, and what it handed down is still known". The bibliographic sequence this library holds records that the Lishui gazetteer was first compiled in 1509 by Chen Xian and Fan Qi and revised in 1525 by Wang Congshan, Fang Yan and Huang Zhida, both efforts judged brief by later readers. That sequence comes from bibliographic description; this library has not read the text of the Wanli gazetteer. On this basis, the biography's "compiled and revised the county gazetteer" corresponds to the 1525 revision, and that Jiajing edition does not survive in the library's sequence — the earliest extant edition is the first printed one of 1579.

**City works.** The *Jiangnan Tongzhi* places him in the sequence of Lishui's wall repairs:

> 嘉靖初知县王从善扩其东隅建石桥以泄水，十年水败东南隅知县张问行修，十七年水复溃，三十六年知县曾震始造石城。
> — *Jiangnan Tongzhi*, juan 20

He widened the eastern corner of the city and built a stone bridge to carry off water. The record gives the act and its consequence but no bridge name or position; the floods of 1531 and 1538 and Zeng Zhen's stone wall of 1557 belong to the sequence, not to this entry.

**The cliff inscription.** The mountains-and-rivers section of juan 5 states directly that the "Fengquan" characters were cut by him:

> 山頂有泉，下注岩石間成瀑布，明嘉靖間知縣王從善鑿石題曰「鳳泉」，構亭其側，今廢。
> — Kangxi *Lishui County Gazetteer*, juan 5

This is the earliest gazetteer text known to this library that links the Fengquan inscription to Wang Congshan, and it is a contemporary gazetteer statement rather than a later guess. Two distinctions matter. First, this passage records Fengquan only, not the Danding or the Wuzun inscription; the claim that all three are his work appears only on the scenic area's website and in the media, and is a tradition, not a record. Second, his biography says nothing about any inscription, and the two passages must not be conflated. The inscription itself is described in the entry on the Wuxiang Mountain cliff inscriptions.

## Conduct

His biography preserves the fullest passage known to this library on how he behaved in office:

> 公暇則過興教寺，翛然自適，不為俗務所拘。顏寺中堂曰「又得」，義取「又得浮生半日閑」也。每有訟，民即寺聽斷，視若無事，蓋超超若世外者。於諸生多所噓拂，士人向風依戀。省荒勸農多得古法。
> — Kangxi *Lishui County Gazetteer*, juan 6

In his leisure he went to the Xingjiao Temple and named its hall "Youde" ("again obtained"), after the line "again obtained half a day of idle life". When there was a lawsuit he heard it at the temple. The biography also records that he encouraged students, that the literati were drawn to him, and that his work urging agriculture followed ancient methods. The temple itself has its own entry; this one records only its connection to him.

## Relation to Lishui

He was not a Lishui native, and enters this library through official service. His traces in Lishui are unusually dense for a Ming official connected to the county: a shrine, a granary, the community oath, a gazetteer, city works, a cliff inscription and the naming of a temple hall, all within Lishui and most of them directly recorded in the Kangxi gazetteer. Three surviving objects in Lishui are tied to him in this library's sources: the Biaozhong shrine site, the Fengquan inscription on Mount Wuxiang, and the "Zhongshan Academy official grant of land and house" stele now in Zhuangyuanfang Park.

## Points of doubt

First, his birth and death years are unknown. The gazetteer records only that he took office in 1524, not his birth, death, or the year he left office.

Second, the end of his term is inconsistent across three sources and cannot be resolved within this entry. The scenic area's website says he served as magistrate of Lishui from 1524 to 1528. The Jiangsu provincial culture-and-tourism report on the Fourth National Cultural Relics Survey says the "Zhongshan Academy official grant of land and house" stele was "erected by Wang Congshan, magistrate of Lishui, in 1554". And the same volume of the Kangxi gazetteer that carries his biography records Gao Chong as appointed in 1528 and Zhang Wenxing as appointed in 1530 — so a successor was in post by 1528, which agrees with "until 1528" and conflicts with a stele in 1554. If he left office in 1528, the 1554 stele comes twenty-six years after his term, and the title "magistrate of Lishui" sits oddly with that. This library has no material that decides which is right, and all three are recorded: the scenic area's dating is not used to fix the end of his term, the stele's date is not used to fix him in office until 1554, and the successor's appointment year is not used to conclude that he had left by 1528 — a successor's arrival only shows that someone took the post that year, and records nothing about when his predecessor handed over.

Third, the shrine is named Biaozhong in one passage of the Kangxi gazetteer and Zhongjie in another. This entry keeps both and does not decide which was the original name.

Fourth, the year of the posthumous name Jieming differs: the Guangxu gazetteer places it in 1525, the common account in the Southern Ming Hongguang period. This entry records only his request for sacrifices; the dating of the posthumous name is handled in the entry on Qi Tai.

Fifth, the style name Fenglin appears only in the scenic area and the media; his biography records no style name. The attribution of all three cliff inscriptions to him is a tradition and is not stated as fact.

Sixth, the site and capacity of the Xiansi granary, and the name and position of the stone bridge at the eastern corner, are not recorded.

Seventh, the Jiajing gazetteer he revised does not survive in this library's sequence, so this entry goes no further than the fact of the revision and the year given in the bibliographic sequence, and says nothing about that gazetteer's content or arrangement.

Eighth, across the seven layers of sources searched: the classical-text layer yields his biography in the Kangxi gazetteer, three further passages in the same book, one in the Guangxu gazetteer and one in the *Jiangnan Tongzhi*, enough to establish his main acts in Lishui. His career before Lishui, his movements afterwards, the year of his examinations, and his relations with contemporaries of the same cohort are recorded in none of the layers. The Guangxu gazetteer passages read by this library contain no biography of him; whether he appears in its category of officials remains to be checked.

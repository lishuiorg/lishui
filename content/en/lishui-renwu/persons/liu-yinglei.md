Liu Yinglei is one of the Ming officials of Lishui listed in juan 6 of the Kangxi *Lishui County Gazetteer*. His biography is among the more concrete of the Ming entries in this volume: one dispute over shifted grain, one land survey, one account of his summons and death on the road, plus the note of a shrine and his inclusion among the distinguished officials. This entry records only what that line records.

## Origin and term

> 刘应雷，江西万安人。由进士，隆庆三年任。
> — Kangxi *Lishui County Gazetteer*, juan 6

He was a native of Wan'an in Jiangxi, entered as a *jinshi*, and was appointed to Lishui in the third year of the Longqing era. The gazetteer gives no office title here, only "appointed"; the era year gives only his arrival, and this entry converts nothing into a common-era year.

## What the record says about his acts

**The dispute over shifted grain.**

> 时傍邑有以粮数千石幸嫁溧水者，公力争，致当道以胜气临之，执益坚，决于纳绶，事竟直。
> — Kangxi *Lishui County Gazetteer*, juan 6

The gazetteer records that at the time people of a neighbouring county were trying to shift several thousand *shi* of grain onto Lishui by luck; Liu Yinglei contested it with all his force, which brought the provincial authorities down on him with arrogance, while he held firmer still and settled the matter by surrendering his seal of office — "settled it by surrendering the seal" is the gazetteer's statement that he risked giving up the post — and the affair was in the end vindicated. "Vindicated" is the gazetteer's conclusion. How the several thousand *shi* were counted, which neighbouring county was meant, who the authorities were, what passed in the dispute and on what basis the matter was judged right — all unrecorded.

**Land survey and the setting of the tax.**

> 又丈量田亩无尺寸漏，因高下制赋克当，见于歌谣。
> — Kangxi *Lishui County Gazetteer*, juan 6

The gazetteer states that his survey of the fields left no fraction uncounted, that he set the tax according to the high and low quality of the ground and hit what was fitting, and that this was expressed in songs. "No fraction uncounted" and "hit what was fitting" are the gazetteer's judgements, and "expressed in songs" is popular reputation, not a verifiable figure. The year of the survey, the acreage and the items of the tax settlement are all unrecorded in this volume.

**Summons, death on the road, and a shrine.**

> 被召去，卒于涂。民怀厥德，立祠祀之，并入名宦。
> — Kangxi *Lishui County Gazetteer*, juan 6

He was summoned away from Lishui and died on the road — "died on the road" means during the journey after leaving. The gazetteer further records that the people kept his virtue in mind, built a shrine to sacrifice to him, and that he was included among the distinguished officials. All three matters are the gazetteer's record: that he settled the grain dispute by risking his post, that he died during the journey out of office, and that a shrine was built and he was entered among the distinguished officials; this entry reports them as recorded and draws no further inference. His year and place of death are unrecorded.

**The record the gazetteer points to.**

> 事详傅御史应祯记。
> — Kangxi *Lishui County Gazetteer*, juan 6

The gazetteer states that the affair is set out in full in a record by Censor Fu. What kind of text that was, where it was kept, and whether it survives are not stated, and this entry supplies nothing. This library has a separate entry recording a magistrate named Fu Yingzhen reviving the village compact in Lishui in the Wanli period, but this line writes only "Censor Fu Yingzhen" without dating him or giving his career, so it cannot be taken as the same man without proof; the two are kept side by side as a doubt.

## Relation to Lishui

He was not a Lishui native and enters this library through official service. What ties him to the county is the grain dispute, the land survey and tax settlement, his summons and death on the road, the shrine built by the people, and the entry "included among the distinguished officials" itself — his shrine and his biography both stand inside this same volume.

His relation to this library's entry on Wang Congshan must be stated: the two biographies come from the same volume (until now only these two entries of this volume had been read, as the source card records), so they are cross-references within one juan; but the affairs they record do not touch each other, and nothing of Liu Yinglei's conduct is supplied from the Wang Congshan entry.

## Points of doubt

First, his birth and death years are unrecorded; we know only that he died on the road after being summoned away, and neither the year nor the place of his death is given.

Second, his departure is given only as "he was summoned away"; the era year gives only his arrival. Nothing is converted, and the length of his term is not inferred.

Third, the gazetteer gives no office title for his Lishui post, only "appointed"; this entry does not write him as a county magistrate.

Fourth, in the grain dispute the counting basis of the several thousand *shi*, the neighbouring county meant, the authorities involved, the course of the dispute and the basis on which it was judged right are all unrecorded.

Fifth, whether "settled it by surrendering the seal" means he actually gave up the office, and what followed, is unrecorded; this entry only reproduces what the gazetteer states.

Sixth, the year of the survey, the acreage and the items of the tax settlement are unrecorded in this volume.

Seventh, "no fraction uncounted", "hit what was fitting" and "expressed in songs" are the gazetteer's judgements and popular reputation.

Eighth, whether "Censor Fu Yingzhen" is the same man as the Wanli-period magistrate Fu Yingzhen recorded elsewhere in this library is not proved by the gazetteer; whether the record he is said to have written survives is likewise unrecorded.

Ninth, the location, date and later history of the shrine are unrecorded.

Tenth, this entry rests on one biography in this volume, which is an online transcription not checked against the punctuated edition or the paper book; searches across the other source layers of this library produced nothing that could add to him.

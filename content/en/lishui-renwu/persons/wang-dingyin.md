Wang Dingyin is one of the Qing officials of Lishui listed in juan 6 of the Kangxi *Lishui County Gazetteer*. His biography records two matters — litigation stirred up by a land survey, and the auditing of long-corrupted county revenue — and closes on the benefit left to those who came after him.

## Origin and term

> 王鼎胤，字六符，山东淄川人。由进士，顺治四年任，初履任即赫赫有神明之誉。
> — Kangxi *Lishui County Gazetteer*, juan 6

He was courtesy-named Liufu, a native of Zichuan in Shandong, entered as a *jinshi*, and was appointed to Lishui in the fourth year of the Shunzhi era, earning at once on arrival a loud reputation as a god among officials. The gazetteer writes only "appointed" and gives no office title; the era year gives only his arrival, and this entry converts nothing. The reputation on arrival is the gazetteer's reported praise, not a verifiable assessment record.

**"Our dynasty" means the Qing.** This volume lists Wang Dingyin together with Luo Jiashi, Yang Guozhen and An Yingsui under the heading "our dynasty". The gazetteer was written by men of the Kangxi period, who called the Qing "our dynasty"; this entry treats him as a Qing official and notes the original wording.

## What the record says about his acts

**The survey and the litigation it invited.**

> 时值丈量，向之以产相授受者率思藉为讼端，公概置不理，民获以安。
> — Kangxi *Lishui County Gazetteer*, juan 6

A land survey was under way, the gazetteer records, and those who had handed property over to one another mostly hoped to use the survey as a ground for litigation; he put all of it aside without entertaining it, and the people had peace. The year, extent and outcome of the survey are unrecorded; "put aside wholesale" is the gazetteer's account of how he handled it, and whether any case files survive is likewise unrecorded.

**Long-corrupted revenue and a strict audit.**

> 邑之财赋积年为奸胥侵蠧，莫可稽核，公严为清算，纤毫无容欺隐，继此者得不受蒙蔽，其贻后人之利多矣。
> — Kangxi *Lishui County Gazetteer*, juan 6

The county's revenue, the gazetteer records, had for years been encroached upon and eaten away by dishonest clerks past all auditing; he audited it strictly, leaving no room for deception or concealment in the finest hair, so that those who came after him were not deceived. "The benefit he left to later men was great" is the gazetteer's assessment of his posthumous effect. What the clerks had done, what the audit found and what followed are all unrecorded.

## Relation to Lishui

He was not a Lishui native and enters this library through official service. What ties him to the county is his appointment in the fourth year of the Shunzhi era, the reputation reported on his arrival, his refusal to open litigation over the survey, his strict audit of long-corrupted revenue, and the effect the gazetteer credits him with for his successors.

## Points of doubt

First, his birth and death years are unrecorded.

Second, the year he left office is unrecorded; the era year gives only his arrival, and this entry neither converts it nor infers the length of his term.

Third, his Lishui office is given only as "appointed", with no precise title.

Fourth, the year, extent and outcome of the survey are unrecorded, as are the cases and their endings.

Fifth, what the dishonest clerks had done and what the audit produced are unrecorded.

Sixth, "a loud reputation as a god among officials", "no room for deception in the finest hair" and "the benefit he left to later men was great" are the gazetteer's reported praise, judgements and assessment.

Seventh, this volume lists him under "our dynasty", meaning the Qing; the original wording is noted above.

Eighth, this entry rests on one biography in this volume, which is an online transcription not checked against the punctuated edition or the paper book; searches across the other source layers of this library produced nothing that could add to him.

Xie Ting is one of the Ming officials of Lishui listed in juan 6 of the Kangxi *Lishui County Gazetteer*. His biography is short: one line of origin and demotion, one line of transfer, one clause of judgement and one clause of aftermath. This entry records only what that line records and supplies no life for him.

## Origin and term

> 谢廷，四川富顺人。由进士，以给事中嘉靖二十年言事谪云南尉，寻调而至。
> — Kangxi *Lishui County Gazetteer*, juan 6

He was a native of Fushun in Sichuan, entered as a *jinshi*, and while serving as a supervising secretary remonstrated in the twentieth year of the Jiajing era and was demoted to a subordinate military rank in Yunnan; "shortly after, he was transferred and arrived" — that is, he was soon reassigned to Lishui. In this line the gazetteer gives no office title for his Lishui post, only "arrived", and gives neither the year of his arrival nor the year he left. The era year given is that of his remonstrance and demotion; this entry does not use it to fix the beginning or the end of his Lishui term, and converts nothing into a common-era year.

## What the record says about his acts

> 裁抑冗滥，济以廉明，去而民怀之。
> — Kangxi *Lishui County Gazetteer*, juan 6

"He cut down the superfluous and the corrupt, and made good his lack with integrity and clarity" is the gazetteer's judgement of his conduct in office; "when he left, the people longed for him" is its account of popular memory after his departure. Neither is a record of a concrete act. The biography lists no act at all: it does not say what he cut down, does not say whether he built or repaired anything, and records no shrine or posthumous commemoration.

## What the same volume says elsewhere

His Lishui title is not in his own biography but in the Loyalty and Integrity biographies of the same volume, under Qi Tai:

> 嘉靖中，知县王从善、谢廷相继为祠祀之，仍请以泰五世孙光裕补博士弟子员守祠，光裕即六龄儿后也。
> — Kangxi *Lishui County Gazetteer*, juan 6

This volume therefore calls him district magistrate in Qi Tai's biography and records that he and Wang Congshan "in succession" held shrine sacrifices for Qi Tai. This entry records his Lishui title as district magistrate on that basis, while stating three things plainly. First, the title comes from the Loyalty and Integrity biographies, not from his own, which writes only "he was transferred and arrived". Second, that biography spells his name as this one does, and not in the longer Guangxu form. Third, it dates the matter only as "in the Jiajing era", a span rather than a year, so this entry uses it neither to fix his arrival nor his departure, and converts nothing. "In succession" says the two men followed one another but gives no year for either, so this entry fixes no order between Xie Ting and Wang Congshan. As for the request that Qi Tai's fifth-generation descendant Guangyu be admitted as a student to keep the shrine, the biography does not say who made it — the clause follows a sentence whose subject is both men — and this entry does not assign it to either; that matter is recorded in this library's entry on Qi Tai.

## Relation to Lishui

He was not a Lishui native and enters this library through official service. What ties him to the county is the statement that he was transferred here, the reputation attributed to him after he left, and the shrine sacrifice he is said to have held for Qi Tai in succession to Wang Congshan in the Loyalty and Integrity biographies of the same volume.

Two links to existing entries must be stated clearly. First, the name appears two ways: both biographies of this volume write it as Xie Ting, while the ritual section of the Guangxu *Lishui County Gazetteer* writes a longer form, Xie Tingchai; this library already keeps both spellings side by side in its entry on Qi Tai, and this entry follows this volume. Second, this library's entries on Qi Tai and on Wang Congshan carry further material connected with him: the shrine sacrifice comes from the Loyalty and Integrity biographies of this volume (above), while the rebuilding of the shrine and the setting of charitable fields come from the Guangxu gazetteer, whose spelling differs from this volume's and is not adopted here.

## Points of doubt

First, his birth and death years are not recorded.

Second, neither his year of arrival in Lishui nor the year he left is recorded. The jiajing-20 year belongs to his remonstrance and demotion and cannot be used to infer the span of his Lishui term; nothing is converted into a common-era year.

Third, his own biography gives no office title for his Lishui post, only "he was transferred and arrived"; the Loyalty and Integrity biographies of the same volume call him district magistrate, and this entry records the title on that basis while noting where it comes from. The Guangxu gazetteer's ritual section writes a district magistrate with the longer spelling, which differs from both biographies of this volume, and is not adopted here. The three spellings and the three places they come from are kept side by side rather than merged into one statement.

Fourth, the transfer from his Yunnan post to Lishui and any career in between are unrecorded.

Fifth, the judgement and the popular longing are the gazetteer's judgement and narration; what they rested on is unrecorded.

Sixth, the name appears in two forms across three places: both biographies of this volume of the Kangxi gazetteer write Xie Ting, both biographies of the Shunzhi gazetteer write the longer form Xie Tingchai, and the ritual section of the Guangxu gazetteer writes the longer form too. The Shunzhi gazetteer is the earlier of the two Qing gazetteers and agrees with the Guangxu one, so it is the Kangxi usage that stands apart; this entry keeps the divergence as a doubt, does not decide which form is his real name, and follows this volume. The Shunzhi text used here is an OCR transcription of a woodblock edition with many misread characters, so its spelling is carried only as a variant for reference and is not adopted.

Seventh, this entry rests on one biography and one passage in the Loyalty and Integrity biographies of this volume of the Kangxi gazetteer, with the Shunzhi gazetteer used for the variant spelling. The Kangxi text is an online transcription and the Shunzhi text an OCR transcription of a woodblock edition; neither has been checked against paper. Searches across the other source layers of this library produced nothing that could add to him.

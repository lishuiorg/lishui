## Scope and method

This selection takes the Ming part of the annalistic volume, from the reduction of Lishui Zhou to a county in 1369 to the petition by local officials in 1644 to restore the commutation of the grain tax. The Ming entries are the densest in the volume and fall into three groups: imperial edicts and the tax and corvée system; natural anomalies and famines; and the works carried out in the county by its magistrates.

The method is that of the three preceding selections. The six entries chosen here cover all three groups and include the cutting of the Yanzhi River in 1393, one of the few works in the county with an exact date.

## The quotations and notes

### 2nd year of the Hongwu era (1369): a zhou becomes a county

> 明太祖洪武二年己酉，改溧水州为县。令立学。
>
> *Translated:* In the second year of the Hongwu era of Emperor Taizu of the Ming, jiyou, Lishui Zhou was changed back into a county, and an order was given to establish a county school.

**Note.** This records the county's return from the Yuan prefecture to county status, the second of the three changes in Lishui's standing. The same entry adds that a school was ordered: the early Ming promoted schooling alongside administrative change, and the annalistic volume habitually records institutions and education together.

### 18th year of the Hongwu era (1385): tax and corvée registers made

> 十八年乙丑，四月，五色云见。造赋役册，定民户上中下三等，凡徭役取验。
>
> *Translated:* In the eighteenth year, yichou, in the fourth month five-coloured clouds were seen. Tax and corvée registers were made, households were graded in three classes, upper, middle and lower, and all corvée service was assigned on the evidence of the register.

**Note.** This is the starting point of the taxation thread on this site: household grade fixed the corvée, and the register was the proof. The gazetteer puts an auspicious sighting and an institution of taxation in one entry, which is a common arrangement in the volume — the anomaly is recorded as it stands, the institution separately. This site keeps the two apart and does not use the one to explain the other.

### 23rd year of the Hongwu era (1390): reserve granaries

> 二十三年庚午，五月，设各县预备仓。八月，遣老人赍钞收籴。
>
> *Translated:* In the twenty-third year, gengwu, in the fifth month reserve granaries were set up in every county. In the eighth month elders were sent with paper money to buy grain in.

**Note.** Reserve granaries were a Ming institution for famine relief, and this entry records both their establishment and the method of buying in grain. The county's granaries continue in the gazetteer with the building of the Xiansi Granary in 1526 and the rebuilding of the Bianmin Granary in 1567, and the three entries together show the line of development.

### 26th year of the Hongwu era (1393): a river cut through the western part of the county

> 二十六年癸酉，八月，太祖命崇山侯李新至溧水，督有司凿县西境一带为河。凿石色赤，时名河胭脂。
>
> *Translated:* In the twenty-sixth year, guiyou, in the eighth month the founding emperor ordered Li Xin, Marquis of Chongshan, to go to Lishui and supervise the relevant offices in cutting a river through the western part of the county. The stone cut was red, and the river was then called the Yanzhi.

**Note.** This records the cutting of the Yanzhi River. Under the same entry the gazetteer reproduces the emperor's order in full and adds the sentence "it is handed down that Li Xin took private advantage of a household, and the founding emperor put him to death". Only the first part is quoted here; the order and the tradition are treated in "Dating the Yanzhi River and Its Method of Excavation" and "On the Name of the Yanzhi River and Yanzhi Hill". Note that the gazetteer writes "the river was then called Yanzhi", a different word order from the later conventional name Yanzhi River; this selection reproduces it as it stands.

### 4th year of the Hongzhi era (1491): Gaochun County detached

> 四年辛亥，析溧水西南境置高淳县。
>
> *Translated:* In the fourth year, xinhai, the southwestern part of Lishui was detached to form Gaochun County.

**Note.** This records the largest detachment of territory the county ever underwent, after which Gaochun and Lishui were separately administered. The gazetteer gives this one sentence and no boundary or reason; the entry on this site relies on the district government's "Historical Development" page as well, and the two agree.

### 37th year of the Jiajing era (1558): the stone wall of Lishui

> 三十七年戊午，知县曾震筑溧水石城。
>
> *Translated:* In the thirty-seventh year, wuwu, the zhixian (county magistrate) Zeng Zhen built the stone wall of Lishui.

**Note.** This records one building of the county's defences, under the magistrate Zeng Zhen. Japanese pirate raids had reached the lower Yangtze in the preceding years, and in 1555 "Japanese pirates attacked the walls of Lishui and the town nearly fell"; the stone wall belongs to that setting. The gazetteer gives neither the extent of the wall nor the labour it took, and nothing is supplied here.

## Source limits and search scope

This selection is drawn from a single source, the first juan of the Kangxi *Lishui County Gazetteer*, the annalistic record, and is taken from a second-hand transcription in an online database, not a photographic copy of the original; the text, dates, personal names and the compilers' own notes are reproduced as the gazetteer has them, with nothing rewritten and nothing supplied that the gazetteer does not record. For the institutional background of Ming taxation, granaries, grain transport and town defences, the *History of Ming*, "Food and Money II", and this site's article on taxation (see "Taxation and Corvée in Ming and Qing Lishui") are available; the selection here remains a quotation and is not used to alter the text of the annals. Of the entry for the Yanzhi River in 1393 only the first part is quoted; the full edict and the tradition that Li Xin was put to death are treated in two separate articles on this site.

Layers searched without result, for this span: official web pages (the district government's "Historical Development" and "Scenic Sites") and the published heritage inventories (layer two) record only the higher-grade remains such as the Yanzhi River and Tiansheng Bridge, which have nothing to do with most of these entries on anomalies and remissions; the academic databases (CNKI, Wanfang, VIP) and the full-text repositories (the National Library's read.nlc.cn and others) yielded no study devoted to the Ming entries of the annals one by one, nor any photographic copy of the gazetteer for checking; the media have nothing to do with this span; the map-survey layer does not apply. All these are "not found in this library's searches", not proof that nothing exists.

## Choices and uncertainties

First, the entries remitting rent, autumn grain and land tax are extremely dense under the Hongwu and Yongle reigns. They are all listed above but not reproduced one by one; what they show is the frequency of remission, which deserves a separate statistical treatment.

Second, the entries on natural anomalies and famine — severe drought, great flood, plague, locusts, earthquake — are numerous and often share an entry with a remission or a relief measure. They are reproduced here without being merged.

Third, the entries for the great famine of 1588 and for the flood-burst of 1595, in which the Fengxian polder alone held, involve the embankment works of the magistrates Du Yunji and Yu Yanxing and belong with the Guangtong Dam thread; see "Polders and the Guangtong Dam in Lishui".

Fourth, the gazetteer treats magistrates unevenly: some are given a name and a single act, others a note at the end of the entry referring the reader to the biographical section. This selection reproduces what stands and does not import detail from elsewhere.
